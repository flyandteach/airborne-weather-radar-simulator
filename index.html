Generic training approximation. This simulator models a generic airborne weather radar for technique training, with a fixed, representative 3.5° beam. Beam width varies by radar and antenna — commonly about 3° on large flat-plate antennas to 10° or more on smaller ones — so the exact tilt technique and beam math for your aircraft come from its own radar manual. The predictive-windshear ranges and thresholds, gain steps and other settings are representative training values — not the specifications of the RDR-4000 or any other specific radar, and the aircraft effects are not a structural model of any specific aircraft.

Echo top vs geometric top: the SIDE view's ECHO TOPS (≥20 dBZ) line marks where reflectivity falls below 20 dBZ; the TOP view's cell labels show each storm's geometric top. The two numbers measure different things and can differ by several thousand feet — an echo top below the geometric top is normal, not a contradiction.
Do it yourself — the pilot formulas (approximations; the table above uses the simulator's exact beam math, so small differences are the curvature term): Beam center ≈ Aircraft alt + 100 × Range(NM) × Tilt(°) 1° of tilt moves the beam about 100 ft per NM of range. Earth curvature raises the beam further at long range — the exact form used here is h = alt + R·tan(tilt) + R²∕(2·k·Rearth), with k = 4⁄3 (the 4⁄3-earth refraction model). Beam top / bottom ≈ Center ± 175 × Range(NM)  ·  Beam thickness ≈ 350 × Range(NM) ft The beam is 3.5° wide, so the half-beam is 1.75° ≈ 175 ft per NM each side of center: about 3,500 ft thick at 10 NM, 14,000 ft at 40 NM. A distant storm can be "in the beam" over a huge altitude band — and a near storm only a thin slice of it. Painting the ground: beam paints ground at range R when Bottom(R) ≤ 0 · Ground range ≈ Altitude ∕ (100 × (downtilt + 1.75)) Downtilt in degrees (positive number when tilted down). Approximation only — curvature pushes the real ground-intercept range a little further out. Tilt to center a storm ≈ (Target alt − Aircraft alt) ∕ (100 × Range(NM)) Target the storm's reflective core — near the freezing level / melting level — not its top.

Key lessons — how to use this for training

Tilt managementThe beam is a flashlight, not a camera. Too high and you overshoot the storm's reflective core (the SIDE view shows the wedge passing over the cell); too low and you undershoot into ground returns. Rule of thumb: tilt so the beam center sits at the storm's peak-reflectivity level — near the freezing level — at the range of interest. Or use the ground-return method: tilt down until ground paints at the top of the display, then raise 1–2°.
Gain disciplineGain adds or subtracts dB from every return. High gain makes weak weather look dangerous and saturates real cores; low gain can erase an embedded CB entirely. Use manual gain briefly to interrogate a cell — then always return to CAL, where the color thresholds mean what the legend says.
Attenuation & PACHeavy rain absorbs radar energy — incrementally, as the beam penetrates the cell, so the attenuation shows inside the storm first: a strongly attenuating cell paints its full core on the near side but its far side is eaten away, collapsing into a lens or crescent shape (compare it with its true outline under Show Truth). Behind a red/magenta core the radar may be blind — a "gap" behind a squall line can be a radar shadow hiding another cell, not a safe hole, and the ground return is blacked out there too: if a cell looks lens-shaped with a black wedge and no ground return behind it, it is attenuating — do not trust gaps behind it. A PAC ALERT arc means exactly that: beyond that azimuth, the picture cannot be trusted. Deviate earlier and wider.
Turbulence limitsDoppler turbulence (magenta speckle in WX+T) only works inside ~40 NM and needs >20 dBZ of return to measure. No speckle at 60 NM does not mean smooth air. And don't confuse the two magentas: in WX, solid magenta is >50 dBZ; in WX+T, speckled magenta is turbulence.
Windshear envelopePredictive windshear only protects you low and close: roughly below 2,300 ft AGL and within a few miles ahead, on takeoff and approach. At cruise it is inhibited and silent — no alert means no detected shear, not no hazard. A red WINDSHEAR alert means go around / escape now, no discussion.
Lightning limitationsStrike data shows where lightning has been, not where the cell is going. Anvil and downwind strikes can occur miles from any radar echo — a "clear" radar with strikes nearby is still a thunderstorm. Use lightning as a supplement to reflectivity, never a replacement.
Storm motion & anvilsCells drift with the steering wind — re-randomized every scenario load and shown in the Scenario and Instructor panels. Watch relative motion, not just range: a cell can drift into (or out of) your path. The anvil is the visible signature of upper winds: it develops over the first minute or so of sim time and spreads downwind, in the direction the storm is moving. Anvil ice is a poor radar target — it paints only light green (~20–28 dBZ) when your beam is up at storm-top altitude, and washes out first when you reduce gain — but it still carries lightning and turbulence risk well away from the core. Deviating upwind keeps you out of the anvil plume and the storm's path; deviating downwind puts you where the storm — and its anvil — are going.
Turbulence, G limits & lightningThe G meter in Flight Controls reads the true air at your actual position and altitude — not what the radar paints. Near a strong cell you get light-to-moderate chop; inside a severe core (supercell, strong squall core, wet microburst parent) the gusts regularly cross the transport limit lines (+2.5 / −1.0 g, amber) and, if you stay, the ultimate lines (+3.75 / −1.5 g, red) — past ultimate the airframe disintegrates. Moderate weather will not break the aircraft; penetrating a severe core will. Fly very close to an electrically active cell and you can also take a lightning strike: the airframe survives it (airliners are built to), but the radar jumbles and resets — settings retained. The radar exists so you never have to find any of this out firsthand.
